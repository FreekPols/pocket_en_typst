## Linear inertia 1, Newton's First Law with a glass of water
Take a glass of water (or another object, breakable objects are preferred) and put it on top of a dry A4 sheet of paper. Pull the paper slowly with a constant velocity to the edge of the table. Then suddenly give a yank. The glass stops. What students feared, does not happen, unless the teacher is clumsy or the bottom of the glass was wet. The glass of water resists the sudden acceleration .... *inertia*! Inertia in essence is resistance to acceleration! One could continue by showing a YouTube video of the famous table cloth demo. Even better, try that yourself. It does need practice and is  beyond a simple pocket demo.

## Acceleration and inertia of water
Just like in the previous experiment, pull a glass or better a transparent rectangular container with water on top of an A4 slowly across the table with constant velocity. The water surface remains horizontal. Now accelerate a little, the water will remain behind and the water surface will slant The angle of the water surface is an indication of the magnitude of the acceleration. However, within a second the effect will be disturbed by waves in a small container.

## Deceleration and inertia of water
Now take a half full glass of water and pull it across the table with constant velocity and then stop the glass. What happens to the water now? The water continues its movement and piles up on the front side of the glass. Again the water resists a change in velocity. Inertia is resistance against acceleration but of course also against deceleration.

## Finding the center of mass
Take a 1 m ruler or broom stick, or whatever. Balance it horizontally on your two index fingers wide apart. Then move the index fingers towards each other. Without any control of the teacher, (s)he could even be blind folded, at any time only one index finger moves, and then the other, they alternate while all the teacher does is moving the hands towards each other. Eventually the fingers meet right under the center of mass of the ruler or stick. The experiment could easily be repeated with the students. They probably have a ruler in their bags or another appropriate object. The explanation: when one finger moves in the direction of the center of mass, an increasing part of the weight of the stick rests on that finger. The friction on the moving finger increases while it decreases on the other finger. Then that finger starts moving and so they alternate until both fingers end up in the center of mass. An interesting variation is when one side of the stick is made heavier by putting something on top or hanging something or using a broom instead of a ruler. Then the center of mass shifts. Ehrlig (1994) described 34 experiments with rulers, both qualitative and quantitative experiments and varying in level from primary to higher education.

## Let your students feel the center of mass
First show with a stick or a ruler or a meter stick or even a broom stick what is the center of mass. Put the stick on the table, pull it slowly outwards over the edge. At a certain point it will start to tip over. If you support the stick at that point with your finger, the two sides will balance. That is the center of mass, it is as if all the mass is concentrated there. Human bodies also have a center of mass.  
> *Stand up all of you. Lean forward. What do you feel? Cramp in your toes? Lean more forward yet, eventually you will have to take a step forward in order not to fall. The center of mass of our body is somewhere in the belly. If that center of mass goes over the toes, then we have to take this step forward to prevent tipping over. Could you lean forward more if you were standing on skies? Yes, your skies are longer toes! Stand up again, lift your leg up (forward), your shoulders will go backward to balance, so that the center of mass is still above your feet. Lift your right leg sideways, now your shoulders will move in opposite direction. Sink through your knees, your brain already knows how to compensate, some parts of the body go forward, some parts go backward*.  

Every time your brain knows automatically how to compensate, that is built-in physics! A longer series of demo's on center of mass along with some spectacular powerpoint pictures is available from the author.

## Static and kinetic friction with stick and scarf
Take a meterstick, or pvc tube, or any other rod such as the end of a broom. Keep it horizontal and hang over it a piece of cloth (towel, hand kerchief, scarf) such that one end is much longer than the other end but it does not move because of static friction (friction when at rest). Then slowly tilt the rod downward a bit until the cloth starts moving. It will then not just move downward, but also sideways. Friction has now become kinetic (friction when moving) and kinetic friction is smaller than static friction thus insufficient to prevent sliding sideways (source: Ineke Frederik, Dutch physics teacher educator).

## Difference between static and kinetic friction
Pull a student bag across the table with a piece of elastic band or a weak spring. Movement will be kind of jerky: stick-slip movement. The elastic band or spring will be longer when the bag is not moving and shorter when it moves, showing that static friction is greater than kinetic friction.

The previous experiment can be used to measure the static coefficient of friction. Measure the angle of the table surface with the horizontal in the position where the glass just does not slide. ## Action versus reaction visualization exercise
To point out the relevant forces and show what is meant by action = - reaction, not to proof it: a rubber band or piece of elastic band can always be found even in a bare classroom. A student may have a rubber band around a lunch box. Stretch it with your finger. The force of the finger on the elastic band equals the force of the elastic band on the finger. Point out that action and reaction always work on different objects. That's why we want students to properly name forces as $F_{finger on elastic band}$ and $F_{elastic band on finger}$. The teacher can also put two students in front of the class, each with one hand pushing the hand of the other student but not moving. Then one student will push so hard that the other has to move back. Is now still $action = - reaction$ or $F _{student_A on student_B} = -F_{student_B on student_A}$? Yes it is, but to understand why student **B** is moving backwards, one has to add the forces **on student B only**: > $F_{student_A on student_B} + F_{friction floor on student_B}$  Remember that F_{friction floor on student_B}$ has a direction opposite to F_{student_B on student_A}$. But if student A cannot hold on and is displaced, it is often a matter of being pushed over, thus of moments of force (torque).
As a 14 year-old Ed once won a tug of war from boys who were stronger and heavier. By keeping his center of mass close to the ground he could not be pulled over easily and could use both hands and feet to increase friction.

## Friction and normal force
Take four textbooks and pile them flat on your hand and hold them up. Then with a finger of the other hand you are going to push the top book. Ask for a prediction, if you push the top book, will only the top book slide, or will two or three books slide along? Why? Then show, only the top book will slide. Friction between the 2$^{\text{nd}}$ and 3$^{\text{rd}}$ book is 2x friction between 1$^{\text{st}}$ (top) and 2$^{\text{nd}}$ as friction is proportional to the normal force.

## Friction and inclined plane
Tilt the instructor's table using an object under one of the legs or using a student to hold one side of the table up. Put anything round on the table (borrow some candy from students, or a coin, or a marble). The round object will accelerate down the table surface. Put a book on the table. What keeps it there? Friction. Increase the tilt, the book does not move yet. Is the magnitude of the friction still the same? Tilt more yet, the book starts moving. Why? Make sure to distinguish between *actual* friction ($mg \sin(\alpha)$) and *maximum* friction ($μ_N = μ_s~mg \cos(\alpha)$). The instructor can even illustrate how friction coefficients are measured by taking the static friction coefficient equal to: $μ_{static} = \tan(\alpha_s)$ where $\alpha_s$ is the tilt angle when the book is just about to slide. The angle can be measured with a mobile phone. Dynamic friction occurs when the book is sliding and in theory the coefficient can be determined at the angle where sliding occurs at constant speed: $μ_{dynamic} = \tan(\alpha_d)$, in practice there may be slip-stick motion.

## Asymmetry in properties, friction of human hair
Ask a hair from a long-haired girl or propose that each student pair takes a hair from one of the two heads. Hold one end between thump and index finger of one hand, and slide with the other thump and index finger along the hair. Then slide in the other direction. ## Pressure and surface area

## Collisions and preventing damage: egg throw
Anybody has a raw (unboiled) egg? Now this is something you might not have in a bare classroom, but then don't forget to bring it from home. Have students hold up a towel, a coat, or any piece of textile, or the curtains of the classroom window, such that most of it is almost vertical but the bottom is folded to create a gutter. Then throw the egg full force into that. It will survive, it will not break. There are two principles at work. 

The force that decelerates the egg is spread over the surface of the egg, rather than applied at one point. Furthermore, the decelerating force is spread in time as the towel or curtain moves along with the egg. 

These two principles spread the braking force in space and time thus reducing the force. This is easy to see in p = mv = Ft. The momentum goes from mv to zero. If the braking time t is greater, the F will be smaller. Same with $E_{kin} = 1/2 mv^{2} = Fs$. The kinetic energy goes from $\frac{1}{2}$ mv2 to zero. If braking takes place over a longer distance s, then the braking force F will be smaller.  The spreading of forces also applies to seatbelts in cars and protective helmets, they spread the force over the body or over the head. Seatbelts also illustrate both the spreading in time and in area of the decelerating force as the belt is wide and moves along with the body, thus lowering the force by spreading it in time and across a wide surface.

b) at a higher speed,

## Relative motion
Student **A** walks with a low constant velocity in front of the class from left to right. student **B** starts later, walks faster and overtakes student **A**. *At the moment that **B** is next to **A**, are the velocities the same or different?* 

Strange question you will think, but some people will answer equal (misconception). In a traffic court case in the USA this opinion was even espoused by a judge. If none of your students go wrong here, then never do this demo again. Also have your students draw position time diagrams for A and B in the same graph (see the previous demo).

## Investigating fall with friction with paper baskets 
From one half A4, it is easy to fold a rectangular basket and staple it and add unused parts of the half A4 into the basket. A sheet of paper swirls down. However, the basket falls with an almost constant velocity. It is possible to convert this demonstration into a laboratory investigation in which students could investigate the influence of area and mass of the paper basket on the time it needs to fall from a certain height. A first approximation could be the formula $t = \frac{h\cdot A}{m}$, so one would predict that doubling height $h$ or doubling area $A$ would result in doubling the fall time $t$. These predictions can be checked by holding an area $2A$ basket at half the height of the area $A$ basket, release them and they should reach the floor at about the same time if the model is correct. For area it appears to fit, or would the air friction scale with $v^2$ rather than $v$? However, for mass $t$ scales with $m^{-\frac{1}{2}}$ No stopwatch needed. An early version of this experiment can be found in @rogers2011physics [pg.167] famous book *Physics for the Inquiring Mind*.

## Projectile motion and relative motion
Walk with constant velocity while throwing a piece of chalk or a coin or a tennis ball straight up. It lands in your hand, *not* behind you. So it had the same horizontal velocity as you when launched! ## Rotational inertia with sticks, brooms, or a hammer
Start with demo 4.1.1 about linear inertia with a glass of water. Inertia is resistance against acceleration. Then put a ballpoint straight up on your hand and try to balance it. You may invite the students to do the same. It is impossible, it will tip over immediately. Now find somewhere in the classroom a meter stick, or a broom, or any stick longer than the ballpoint. Put it straight up on the hand and try to balance it. That works quite well. The object resists rotational acceleration ..... rotational inertia! The longer the object, the greater the rotational inertia, the easier it is to balance it. If then on top of the object there is an extra weight, like with a broom upside down, then it becomes easier yet. If you can find a hammer, you can show that it is easy to balance if the weight is up and difficult if the weight is down (metal top part on your finger). The taller the object and the farther the center of mass is from the pivot point on the hand, the greater the rotational inertia. Rotational inertia is rarely included in high school physics, but that is no reason not to demonstrate it! Source: my late former student Alfredo Guirit, from Tagbilaran, Bohol, Philippines.

## Circular motion with water
Fill a glass half with water. Move the glass in a circle. The water rises against the side of the glass. The water has the tendency to go straight, the side of the glass exerts a centripetal force on the water to force the water particles into circular motion.

## Torque and distance from axis
Again, use the door. Push with your finger at the end, the door moves easily but the finger has to move a great distance to turn the door through 90$^o$. Now push close to the hinges. ## Stretched arm and bag of books
Take a student's bag with books, hold it at arm's length and then hold it next to your body. Which one is easier? The moment of force, the product of force times arm, is greatest at arm's length and we can feel that very well. Let the students do this themselves with their bags.

## Lifting bend and stretched legs while sitting
All students sit on their chair. Lift one knee a bit up from the chair. No problem. Now stretch the leg and again try to lift the knee from the chair. It is a lot more difficult this time. The moment of force (force times distance from hip to center of mass of the leg) is much greater now.

## Balloon rocket and  momentum
Let a blown balloon go, it becomes a rocket but one that moves uncontrollable in all directions. It is nicer to use an elongated balloon with a straw taped on lengthwise. Pull a fish string of a few meters long through the straw and let two students hold it up. That is then a launching path for the balloon. Now shift the balloon to the start of the line and let go. The balloon moves as a rocket to the other end of the line. The rocket principle: air (or rocket exhaust) is expelled with high speed to the left, the rocket is accelerated to the right, required by conservation of momentum. This is a nice experiment for an evening with parents or another event audience. Make sure to use a fresh balloon and blow it up only seconds before the demonstration. Ed once had a balloon blown some 20 minutes before the demo and it had already lost much of its elasticity at the start of the demo.
Freek once attached a small skyrocket (fire works) with a straw to a fish line strung up across the classroom. At the end of the rocket was a sensor with a wire connected with a laptop. That way the motion and force of the rocket could be measured.


## Colliding coins
Two coins A and B (could be Euro's) are touching. Another coin C is launched towards B while B is pressed firmly against the table (figure 4.33). Yet the momentum is passed perfectly from C to A in spite of pressing B very hard against the table.  Surprising. Coin A can even be put under an angle with the line CB. The transmission mechanism for the momentum transfer must be a wave (Subagyo & Berg, 1992). The experiments can also be made quantitative, see a recent article of Barbara Rovšek in The Physics Teacher (Rovšek & Žigon, 2021).

## Tensile and shear stress with a piece of chalk
Take a piece of chalk. Pull on the two sides, that creates tensile stress. The chalk breaks with a very nice flat break surface perpendicular to the length of the chalk. Now take another piece of chalk and twist the ends in opposite directions. We now have shear stress. The chalk breaks with a very different break surface. Try it, the effect is crystal clear and can be found Feynman's Lectures of Physics II p39-9.
